Triangulation and simple stereo

Depth from two cameras with known or partly known parameters: rays, disparity, window matching, the disparity map, and following a pixel to its 3D point.

1One ray vs two rays

A calibrated camera turns a pixel into a ray: the point lies somewhere on it, but the depth is unknown. A second camera, shifted sideways by the baseline \(b\), gives a second ray. The two rays intersect at the 3D point. This is triangulation.

2Why a wider baseline is better

Every pixel measurement has a small error, so each ray is really a thin wedge. The orange region is where the point can be. With a small baseline the wedges cross at a shallow angle and depth is uncertain; with a large baseline the region shrinks.

$$d = \frac{b\,f_x}{z}$$

Larger \(b\) gives a larger \(d\), so a 1-pixel error matters less.

3The matching window

One pixel is a single brightness value and is not unique along the row. So we take the pixel together with its neighbours. The window's values form a fingerprint of the centre pixel. Like a CNN kernel it gathers neighbours and the result belongs to the centre pixel, but there are no learned weights: the left window is compared with right windows.

4Comparing windows: SAD, SSD, NCC

Move the shift slider and watch the three metrics. Then switch on the brightness change: SSD can pick the wrong shift, while NCC still finds the right one because it compares the pattern's shape, not its absolute brightness.

$$\mathrm{SAD}(d) = \sum_{(i,j)\in W} \bigl|\,I_l(u+i,\,v+j) - I_r(u+i-d,\,v+j)\,\bigr| \quad\text{(min)}$$$$\mathrm{SSD}(d) = \sum_{(i,j)\in W} \bigl(I_l(u+i,\,v+j) - I_r(u+i-d,\,v+j)\bigr)^2 \quad\text{(min)}$$$$\mathrm{NCC}(d) = \frac{\sum \bigl(W_l - \bar W_l\bigr)\bigl(W_r - \bar W_r\bigr)}{\sqrt{\sum \bigl(W_l - \bar W_l\bigr)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum \bigl(W_r - \bar W_r\bigr)^2}} \in [-1,\,1] \quad\text{(max)}$$

\(W_l\) is the left window, \(W_r\) the right window shifted by \(d\), \(\bar W\) the mean of a window.

5One row, step by step

This is the actual loop. For one pixel the window slides over every shift \(d\) along the same row, each shift gives one cost, the smallest wins, and its index is written into the map. Then the next pixel in the row, then the next row.

$$d^{*}(u,v) = \arg\min_{d}\; C(u,\,v,\,d), \qquad D[v,\,u] = d^{*}$$

6From costs to a disparity map

Click any pixel to see its cost bars and the winning shift. Run it on every pixel to watch the full map appear, including the typical errors: borders where the window does not fit, the left edge, and occlusion next to the box.

$$z = \frac{b\,f_x}{d^{*}}$$

7Every pixel has its own d

The whole image becomes a map with one disparity per pixel. Toggle to the depth map and click a pixel to follow it all the way to a 3D point. The formulas are the same for every pixel; only \(u\), \(v\) and \(d\) change.

8When the camera is also shifted in y

If the right camera is shifted vertically too, the match leaves the row and lies on a slanted line. With a known \(b_y\) we search along that line. With an unknown \(b_y\) we first match a few strong points in 2D, read the direction from their parallel arrows, estimate \(b_y\), and then search along the estimated line.

$$\frac{v_l - v_r}{u_l - u_r} = \frac{f_y\,b_y}{f_x\,b_x}, \qquad z = \frac{f_x\,b_x}{u_l - u_r}$$

9Search along the epipolar line

Click a point in the left image. The fundamental matrix gives its epipolar line in the right image, and the window slides only along that line.

$$\begin{bmatrix}u_l & v_l & 1\end{bmatrix} F \begin{bmatrix}u_r \\ v_r \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a\,u_r + b\,v_r + c = 0$$
